# Mutual hand-hand grasps: does anyone already have a taxonomy?

## The question

At Zack's Dance Lab we already worked out the *connections* between two dancers' hands. Four
hands, and you ask which ones are joined to which: that's a set partition of a 4-element set, and
there are exactly B(4) = 15 of them. That layer is done. It answers "who holds whom."

It does not answer "*how* do they hold." A handshake hold and a West Coast Swing hook grip can be
the very same connection — leader's right to follower's left, one join — and feel like completely
different objects in your hands. One is palm-to-palm with the thumb wrapped; the other is two bent
fingers offered like a hook that the follower drapes over from above. Same partition. Different
grip. The connection layer is blind to that difference.

So the question for this survey: **has anyone, in any field, built a taxonomy of the grips
themselves — of how two hands mutually clasp?** Not the forces through the grip, not which hand
holds which, but the geometry and mechanics of the mutual grasp.

## The one-paragraph answer

No. Nobody has. The gap is real, and it is specific. The grasp taxonomies everyone cites — Napier,
Cutkosky, Feix — classify *one* hand gripping a *passive object*, and every one of their assumptions
breaks when the "object" is another hand that is gripping you back. Dance's own codifications (NDCA,
Laird, ISTD, CALLERLAB) stop exactly where our set-partition work already stopped: at which-hand-
holds-which. The human-human haptics literature — handshakes, hand-holding, partnered-stepping
robots — treats the mutual grip as a black-box force channel and measures newtons, never shape. The
closest living things to a mutual-grasp taxonomy are in sport: acroyoga's named weight-bearing
holds, the BJJ/wrestling clasp vocabulary, arm wrestling's referee-assembled palm-to-palm grip, and
judo's peer-reviewed grip-coding method. Each is close in one way and short in another. Their union
nearly spans what a dance grip taxonomy would need — but nobody has assembled it, and none of them
covers dance's light, releasable, rotate-mid-turn holds. This document collects the evidence and
sketches what an assembly might look like.

## Dance pedagogy & dance science

**NDCA — "Definitions of Allowable Positions and Holds" (v3).** National Dance Council of America,
undated competition rules document.
<https://www.ndca.org/pdf/Definition%20Positions%20and%20Holds%20v3.pdf> **[verified, full PDF]**
The ceiling of formal ballroom hold classification. Defines a "Hold" as the point(s) of contact
between partners and enumerates Traditional Ballroom Hold, Frame Hold, Hand Holds (Single R-to-L /
L-to-R, Double, Handshake R-to-R / L-to-L), Shadow/Cuddle Holds, Crossed Hand Holds, Hammerlock,
Hand(s) to Body, No Hold. Every distinction is *which-hand-to-which-hand* — the exact set-partition
level ZDL already formalized — with zero content on finger, thumb, or palm mechanics. Bonus: it
treats Position, Proximity (Contact/Close/Extended/Apart/Away), Feet, and Timing as independent
axes, a nice precedent for keeping hold/position/proximity orthogonal.

**NDCA — "Vocabulary of Dance Positions, Holds and Proximities."**
<https://www.ndca.org/pdf/Holds%20and%20Positions.pdf> **[search-verified]**
Companion chart mapping which holds are legal at Bronze/Silver/Gold. Same vocabulary as above;
confirms the ballroom establishment stops at hand-pairing plus body-contact-point.

**Walter Laird — *The Laird Technique of Latin Dancing* (1961; 8th ed. in print).**
<https://www.wikidancesport.com/wiki/technique-of-latin-dancing-walter-laird/> (secondary)
**[dead end / partial: book not freely online; wikidancesport fetch 403]**
The canonical Latin technique manual. Secondary sources confirm it codifies holds/positions per
figure (Close Hold, Open Hold, Fan) and Laird does describe the normal hold's clasp in prose — but
as a single prescribed clasp, not a classification of alternatives. Same story for the ISTD *Ballroom
Technique* and WDSF technique books: positional hold vocabulary, no public evidence of a clasp-level
taxonomy. Follow-up: check the physical books.

**"Salsa Dance Hand Holds" — The Salsa Handbook.**
<https://salsahandbook.github.io/output/salsa-handholds.html> **[verified]**
Nine named holds — Parallel, Half Open (R-to-L / L-to-R), Handshake, Reverse Handshake, Crosshold,
Reverse Crosshold, crossed-parallel variants — annotated with over/under stacking (e.g. "LRH/LLH"
for which hand is on top). Purely hand-pairing plus stacking order; no finger/palm mechanics. The
over/under distinction is one small step past the set-partition level (an ordering on the crossed
double holds).

**Salsa Forums — "Salsa handholds" thread.**
<https://www.salsaforums.com/threads/salsa-handholds.37483/> **[unverified: fetch 403; content from
search snippets]**
Community grip slang richer than any formal source: "pistol grip" (leader's fingers point at the
follower like a pistol, thumbs up, middle two fingers bent 90°, follower connects palms-down),
"spiderman grip," "the cup" / "climb the wall," "gun." Evidence that dancers *do* have grip-level
vocabulary — it just lives in forums and studio slang, not literature.

**"Handhaltung" (Hand Position) — west-coast-swing.net.**
<https://west-coast-swing.net/tanzen/handhaltung/> **[verified]**
The most mechanically explicit grip description found anywhere. Leader's thumb up, index extended,
middle and ring fingers bent ~90° into a hook, pinky relaxed and force-free, hand tilted slightly
down; follower grasps the two hooked fingers loosely from above with all fingers but the thumb. It
even analyzes the grip's degrees of freedom: stiff in pull/push, compliant up/down/left/right. A real
single-grip mechanical spec — but it names no grip and cites no taxonomy. (This is essentially the
"pistol grip," described in full without using the name.)

**David Drysdale — "Hand Holds" (Lindy Hop notes, lurklurk.org).**
<https://www.lurklurk.org/lindyhop/m_hand_holds.html> **[verified]**
Four holds (Left-to-Right, Right-to-Right, plus turn variants) *and* grip mechanics: leader's left-
hand thumb on top, index + middle bent, ring + little folded into palm; follower curls her fingers
around the two offered fingers; describes how her hand rotates during turns (little finger uppermost
overhead). One of only two verified written sources (with the WCS page above) that specify finger-
level mechanics. Personal notes, not peer-reviewed.

**Victor Eijkhout — "Arm Tone, Connection, Force Level and Body Leads" (lead/follow essays).**
<https://www.eijkhout.net/lead_follow/wcs_connection.html>,
<https://www.eijkhout.net/lead_follow/arm_tone.html> **[search-verified]**
Long analytical essays by a scientist-dancer. Treats connection as forces and tone
(compression/leverage, ounces of pressure), not grip morphology. The closest thing to physics-minded
dance writing pre-ZDL, and it confirms the analytical tradition focuses on force, not grasp topology.

**Swing It! (Australia) — "Beginner Tip: Hand Holds"; dogpossum.org connection essays.**
<https://swingit.com.au/tips-beginners-1/>,
<https://dogpossum.org/2018/07/how-i-describe-connection-in-lindy-hop/> **[search-verified]**
Lindy pedagogy: teaches the "hook" (curl fingers in, don't squeeze; warns against the beginner "death
grip"; straight fingers give the lead nothing to hook). Sam Carroll (dance scholar/teacher) discusses
connection pedagogy at length but — tellingly — with no grip taxonomy. Confirms "hook" as the
pan-swing informal grip term.

**Tango Voice — "Variations in the Tango Embrace: The Evidence from Buenos Aires Milongas" (2014).**
<https://tangovoice.wordpress.com/2014/05/20/variations-in-the-tango-embrace-open-embrace-and-close-embrace-styles-of-tango-the-evidence-from-buenos-aires-milongas/>
**[search-verified]**
Quasi-scholarly observational study counting embrace variants at BsAs milongas: High/Low/Mid-level
Embrace, V-Embrace, with frequency data by role. Classifies embrace geometry and arm placement,
including joined-hand height — but treats the actual hand clasp as a given, no clasp variants
recorded. The best example of *empirical* hold classification in a social dance, and it still doesn't
descend to grip level.

**CALLERLAB definitions + Rich Reel, "Square Dance Hand Holds" (1999).**
<http://www.all8.com/sd/dancing/handhold.htm> **[verified]**; CALLERLAB Basic/Mainstream defs e.g.
<https://www.ceder.net/oldcalls/view.php?what=clb&style=book> **[search-verified]**
The closest thing to an explicit grip *repertoire* in any dance genre. Modern Western Square Dance
standardizes a grip per call: Forearm grip (hand past wrist not past elbow, fingers/thumb held close
— official for Allemande Left), Hands Up / crossed-palm ("palms together, fingers up," thumbs-locked
gentle clasp), Handshake hold, Crossed hands, Crossed fingers (light touch). Reel describes five
grips with mechanics, regional variation, and per-call usage. Descriptive rather than generative —
organized by custom and call, not by any principle — but it proves genre-level grip standardization
exists.

**Wikipedia — "Handhold (dance)"; "Glossary of partner dance terms"; "Connection (dance)."**
<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handhold_(dance)>,
<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connection_(dance)> **[verified]**;
Glossary **[search-verified]**
"Handhold (dance)" lists ~20 named holds (butterfly, skaters, sweetheart/cuddle, hammerlock, basket,
promenade, varsouvienne...) — all body-position level, one citation, no clasp mechanics. "Connection
(dance)" classifies hand-arm connection *by force* only: tension / compression / neutral. The
encyclopedic layer mirrors the pedagogy: holds = positions, connection = forces, grips = unnamed.

**Sommer Gentry & Roderick Murray-Smith — swing dance haptics (2003); Gentry PhD thesis (MIT, 2005).**
"Haptic Dancing" (IEEE SMC 2003); "Got Rhythm? Haptic-Only Lead and Follow Dancing" (Eurohaptics
2003); *Dancing Cheek to Cheek* (MIT PhD thesis, 2005).
<https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/33207> **[thesis URL blocked, HTTP 405; existence verified via
MIT News <https://news.mit.edu/2004/dancerobot> and citation trail]**
Foundational lindy-hop haptics: blindfolded followers decode the move vocabulary from hand forces
alone; swing modeled as a finite state machine. Formalizes the *signal* through the hands, not the
grasp — the connection is a force channel of given impedance. Direct kin to ZDL (dancer-scientist
formalizing swing) and the clearest evidence that even formal dance science abstracted the grip away.

**Joseph Daniel DeMers — "Frame matching and ΔPTED," *Research in Dance Education* 14(1):71–80,
2013.** <https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14647893.2012.688943>;
ERIC <https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1012371> **[search-verified]**
The only peer-reviewed *pedagogical* framework found for partner-dance connection: codifies
connection as changes in Posture, Tone, Tension, Energy, Direction (ΔPTED) plus "frame matching." A
genuine prior formalization from inside the swing/blues community — but its primitives are again
force/tone qualities; grips are out of scope.

**Sawers, Bhattacharjee, McKay, Hackney, Kemp, Ting — "Small forces that differ with prior motor
experience...," *J. NeuroEngineering & Rehabilitation* 14:8, 2017.**
<https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12984-017-0217-2> **[search-verified]**
Expert-dancer dyads in partnered stepping: interaction forces are small (~5–12 N), differ with dance
experience, and suffice to communicate movement goals. The Ting-lab program (with Hackney's
Parkinson's-tango work) is the flagship of partner-dance haptics — and it uses a fixed,
uncharacterized hand/arm coupling. Grip type is a controlled-away variable, never a studied one.
Companion: Chen et al., *PLOS ONE* 10(5):e0125179, 2015 — expert dancers rate robot interaction best
when forces are lowest.

**Ann Hutchinson Guest & Joukje Kolff — *Advanced Labanotation, Issue 5: Hands and Fingers* (Dance
Books, ~2003).** <https://www.amazon.com/Hands-Fingers-Advanced-Labanotation-Hutchinson/dp/1852730862>
**[unverified content; print book, listing confirmed]**
The strongest candidate for a *formal* system that could encode grips: Labanotation has contact
hooks, grasp symbols, and a whole volume on hand/finger notation. But I found no published
application of it to partner-dance handhold classification. General refs (Wikipedia "Labanotation,"
Dance Notation Bureau <https://www.dancenotation.org/labanotation-fundamentals/>) confirm
contact/grasp notation exists; nobody has used it to enumerate holds. This is the key open follow-up:
the notation could describe grips, it just never has.

*Dance dead ends (searched, little or nothing found):*
- **"Taxonomy/classification of dance handholds" as a direct query** — returns only the Wikipedia
  lists and NDCA PDFs. No academic or pedagogical work formally taxonomizes hand-hand grips in dance.
  This is the gap.
- **Journal of Dance Medicine & Science** — searched for partner hand connection / grip studies;
  nothing on hand grips (partner-dance content there is lower-limb injury and ballet-lift
  biomechanics). No grip-level study located.
- **Skippy Blair / Universal Unit System (WCS)** — codifies rhythm units, not grips, despite WCS
  folklore crediting her circle with "pistol grip."
- **Brazilian zouk / bachata / kizomba** — abundant connection pedagogy (stretch/recoil, leverage),
  named grip vocabulary absent. Sites say "different ways to hold hands" and never name them.
- **Historical dance manuals** (19th-c. ballroom, Playford country-dance, allemande holds) — likely
  contain hold descriptions; out of reach this pass, flagged as follow-up.

## Grasp taxonomies in robotics & biomechanics

**J. R. Napier — "The prehensile movements of the human hand," *J. Bone & Joint Surg. (Br)*
38-B(4):902–913, 1956.** <https://boneandjoint.org.uk/Article/10.1302/0301-620X.38B4.902>
The origin point. Reduces all human grasps to **power grip** (object clamped between flexed fingers
and palm, thumb buttressing in-plane) vs **precision grip** (object pinched between finger pads and
opposed thumb pad, held off the palm), plus combinations. Argues the *intended activity*, not object
shape alone, dictates the grip — an early hint that grasp is relational, not purely geometric.
Single hand, inert object.

**M. R. Cutkosky — "On grasp choice, grasp models, and the design of hands for manufacturing tasks,"
*IEEE T-RA* 5(3):269–279, 1989.**
<https://bdml.stanford.edu/twiki/pub/Seabed/LiteratureReview/Cutkosky_-_1989_-_On_Grasp_Choice_Grasp_Models_and_the_Design_of_Hands_for_Manufacturing_Tasks.pdf>
Sixteen grasps in a hierarchical tree: split power vs precision, then branch by object geometry and
size, with qualitative measures — compliance, connectivity, grasp isotropy, slip resistance. The
canonical "grasp choice depends on task + object + dexterity" tree. Rigid manufactured parts, one
hand.

**T. Feix et al. — "The GRASP Taxonomy of Human Grasp Types," *IEEE T-HMS* 46(1):66–77, 2016.**
<https://www.eng.yale.edu/grablab/pubs/Feix_THMS2016.pdf> **[PDF live but returned binary on fetch;
dimensions confirmed via secondary sources]**
The modern synthesis: 33 grasp types on four dimensions — (i) **opposition type** (pad / palm /
side); (ii) **virtual-finger assignment** (functional grouping of digits acting as one unit);
(iii) **power / intermediate / precision**; (iv) **thumb position** (abducted vs adducted). Collapses
to 17 if only hand shape (not object) matters. This is the richest dimension set and the standard
vocabulary — and its assumptions (single hand, rigid object, clean gripper→object role) are exactly
the ones that break for hand-on-hand. The best scaffold to extend, precisely because it fails
cleanly.

**N. Kamakura et al. — "Patterns of static prehension in normal hands," *Am. J. Occupational
Therapy* 34(7):437–445, 1980.** <https://ajot.aota.org/article.aspx?articleid=1889836>
Empirical OT study: 7 adults, 98 objects, photographed from 5 directions, contact areas mapped. 14
patterns in 4 categories (5 power, 4 intermediate, 4 precision, 1 no-thumb hook). Its distinguishing
dimension is **contact-area topology** — which hand surfaces touch — directly useful for dance holds,
where *which surfaces touch* defines the hold. Single hand, inert object.

**J. M. Elliott & K. J. Connolly — "A classification of manipulative hand movements," *Dev. Med. &
Child Neurology* 26(3):283–296, 1984.**
<http://graphics.cs.cmu.edu/nsp/course/16899-s16/papers/Elliott1984.pdf>
Classifies within-hand (intrinsic) manipulation into simple synergies, reciprocal/complex synergies,
and sequential patterns, splitting *palmar grips that immobilise* from *digital patterns that permit
motion*. Relevant because a dance hold is dynamic — it must both immobilise (support/lead) and permit
motion (turns) at once.

**I. M. Bullock, R. R. Ma, A. M. Dollar — hand-centric manipulation taxonomy, 2011/2013.**
<https://ian-bullock.com/pubs/BullockManipPreprintToH2012.pdf>
A *motion-centric* taxonomy: five binary descriptors — contact? / prehensile? / object motion? /
within-hand motion? / motion-at-contact? — give 15 mutually exclusive classes. Uniquely separates
contact from prehension from motion, which is exactly the axis set a mutual grasp stresses: both
hands are simultaneously contacting AND moving AND prehending. The cleanest tools for describing a
hold that must contact, prehend, and permit motion all at once.

**I. A. Kapandji — thumb opposition score, 1986.** <https://www.physio-pedia.com/Kapandji_Score>
Not a taxonomy — an **opposition-range metric**: thumb-tip reach scored 0–10 across landmarks on the
same hand. Included because "opposition" is the recurring primitive across every taxonomy above;
Kapandji operationalizes it as a measurable scale. Intra-hand only — no notion of opposing another
person's hand.

**A. Stival et al. — "A quantitative taxonomy of human hand grasps," *J. NeuroEngineering &
Rehabilitation* 16:28, 2019.** <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6377750/>
Data-driven re-derivation using EMG + kinematics + forces to build a *measured* hierarchy rather than
an expert-defined one. Best used as a review: it re-summarizes Napier, Cutkosky, Feix, Kamakura,
Bullock and their dimensions, and confirms the field's shared assumptions (rigid object, single
agent).

**F. Krebs & T. Asfour — "A Bimanual Manipulation Taxonomy," *IEEE RA-L* 7(4):11031–11038, 2022.**
<https://h2t.iar.kit.edu/pdf/Krebs2022.pdf> **[PDF live but binary on fetch; dimensions from
abstract/secondary sources]**
**Citation correction:** an earlier brief guessed "Krebs & Feix" — *that pairing does not exist.* The
correct authors are **Krebs & Asfour** (KIT H2T lab); Feix is the single-hand GRASP taxonomy above.
Classifies bimanual tasks by inter-arm coordination (coordinated vs uncoordinated), spatiotemporal
dependency, and motion symmetry (symmetric/asymmetric), with coordinated motions sub-typed
parallel/blended/serial. The closest formal precedent for two effectors acting together — but the two
hands act jointly on a *third* external object, not on each other.

**"A Taxonomy of Self-Handover," arXiv:2504.04939, 2025.** <https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.04939>
Classifies transferring an object between one's *own* two hands. Makes **role a variable** (holding →
passing → receiving hand) — interesting — but the two hands still coordinate around an external
object rather than gripping each other.

**"Human–robot object handover: recent progress and future direction," review, 2024.**
<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667379724000032>
Reviews handover as a giver/receiver joint action with pre-handover and transfer phases. Comes
closest to *reciprocal force between two agents*: during transfer there's a shared-object, dual-grasp
interval where load is negotiated. But the goal is object *transfer* — grip transient, roles
asymmetric and sequential — not a sustained mutual hold.

**"T-DOM: A Taxonomy for Robotic Manipulation of Deformable Objects," arXiv:2412.20998, 2024.**
<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.20998>
Extends manipulation taxonomy to deformable objects (cloth, cable, dough). Relevant for one failing
assumption — rigidity. Shows the field already knows rigid-object taxonomies break for soft objects;
a partner's hand is deformable AND actuated, a case even T-DOM doesn't cover (its deformables are
passive).

*Robotics dead ends:*
- **"Krebs & Feix bimanual taxonomy"** — does not exist. Flagged so nobody cites a phantom. The real
  paper is Krebs & Asfour 2022.
- **No taxonomy for two hands mutually gripping each other** — confirmed gap in robotics and
  biomechanics. Nearest neighbors (bimanual, handover, self-handover) all stop short of a
  role-symmetric, sustained, mutually-actuated grip. The absence is itself a finding.
- Multi-object grasping (Sun et al., USF RPAL 2022) and VR-Grasp (2024) surfaced but are peripheral;
  not annotated.

## Handshake & human-human haptics

**Prasad, Stock-Homburg & Peters — "Human-Robot Handshaking: A Review," *Int. J. Social Robotics*,
2021.** <https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12369-021-00763-z> **[verified, read in full]**
The keystone. Surveys ~90 handshake papers and splits the interaction into four phases: reaching,
**grasping**, shaking, termination. The "grasping" phase is treated entirely as a
force/pressure/closure problem — contact force, grip strength, degree of closure, impedance control —
never as a geometric taxonomy of how fingers and palm interlock. Reports (via Knoop et al.) that
"there is possibly no fixed grasping location for a handshake" and that grip configuration is
essentially fixed (palm-to-palm), so only force varies. The strongest single piece of evidence for
the black-box-force verdict.

**Melnyk & Hénaff — "Physical Analysis of Handshaking Between Humans," *Int. J. Social Robotics*,
2019** (with 2014 IEEE AIM precursor). **[search-verified]**
Instrumented handshakes (glove with IMU + 6 palm force sensors) analyzing mutual synchronisation
across social contexts and gender pairings. Reports duration, wrist frequency (~3.5–4 Hz), grip
strength (~6 N). Measures forces and timing, not grip geometry.

**Tagne, Hénaff & Gregori — "Measurement and Analysis of Physical Parameters of the Handshake...,"
*IEEE/RSJ IROS 2016*.** **[search-verified]**
Extends the glove-sensor approach to elbow/shoulder IMUs; grip strength varies by context (lowest for
sympathy, highest for congratulation). Force/kinematics, no configuration taxonomy.

**Knoop, Bächer, Wall, Deimel, Brock, Beardsley — "Handshakiness: Benchmarking for Human-Robot Hand
Interactions," *IEEE/RSJ IROS 2017*.** **[search-verified via review]**
The closest anyone comes to studying grip *geometry*: measures contact area, contact pressure, and
finger contact positions in weak/normal/strong handshakes. Key finding — large variation in finger
positions on the *back* of the hand but little on the palm side ("almost the whole palm is held") —
so they conclude there's no single fixed grasp location and characterize grip coarsely. Even the one
paper that looks at contact shape concludes configuration is nearly invariant and builds no typology.
Its **contact-area method is exactly what a dance grip taxonomy would need** — pointed at variety
instead of at the near-invariant handshake.

**Avelino et al. — "Towards Natural Handshakes for Social Robots," *Paladyn J. Behavioral Robotics*,
2018** (and "The Power of a Handshake," IROS 2018). **[search-verified]**
Three grasping models distinguished by *degree of hand closure* (strong/medium/weak) via finger-joint
force sensors, then closed-loop force control. The "types" are force levels, not geometric
configurations.

**Karniel, Nisky, Avraham et al. — "A Turing-Like Handshake Test for Motor Intelligence,"
EuroHaptics 2010** (+ Nisky 2012 *Presence*, Avraham 2012 *IEEE T-Haptics*).
<https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10464> **[search-verified]**
Introduces the Model Human-Likeness Grade on a 1-D force-controlled stylus. Entirely a
dynamics/force-trajectory framing — the grip is literally reduced to a 1-DOF stylus, the ultimate
black-box force channel.

**Stock-Homburg, Peters et al. — "Evaluation of the Handshake Turing Test for Anthropomorphic
Robots," *ACM/IEEE HRI 2020 Companion*.** <https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10464> **[search-verified]**
Blindfolded participants compared a soft-silicone android hand vs a real human hand (11/15 spotted
the robot first try). About human-likeness of the physical interface and static grip *feel*, not
configuration classification.

**Chaplin, Phillips, Brown, Clanton, Stein — "Handshaking, Gender, Personality, and First
Impressions," *J. Personality & Social Psychology* 79(1), 2000.**
<https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/psp791110.pdf> **[search-verified]**
The canonical social-psych handshake study: 112 students, handshakes coded on strength, vigor,
duration, eye contact, completeness of grip. "Completeness of grip" is the nearest thing to a
configuration variable — but it's a quality rating (full vs partial palm engagement), not a geometric
type. Grip as a scalar quality dimension.

**Nagy, Farkas, Guy, Stafylarakis — "Effects of Handshake Duration on Other Nonverbal Behavior,"
*Perceptual & Motor Skills* 127(1):52–74, 2020.** **[search-verified, cited in Prasad review]**
Timing/dynamics of the handshake and downstream nonverbal effects. Timing focus.

**Ganesh, Takagi, Osu, Yoshioka, Kawato, Burdet — "Two Is Better Than One," *Scientific Reports*
4:3824, 2014.** <https://www.nature.com/articles/srep03824> **[search-verified]**
Foundational dyadic-haptic paper: two people physically connected by elastic bands while tracking a
target both improve, each inferring the other's goal via haptic coupling. The connection is an
elastic spring — a pure force channel; the hand grasp itself is not studied or classified.

**Takagi, Ganesh, Yoshioka, Kawato, Burdet — "Physically Interacting Individuals Estimate the
Partner's Goal...," *Nature Human Behaviour* 1:0054, 2017.**
<https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-017-0054> **[search-verified]**
Mechanistically shows partners exchange information through coupling force to estimate each other's
target. Coupling modeled as a mechanical connection; grip geometry irrelevant to the model.

**Sawers & Ting — "Perspectives on Human-Human Sensorimotor Interactions...," *J. NeuroEngineering &
Rehabilitation* 11:142, 2014.**
<https://jneuroengrehab.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1743-0003-11-142> **[search-verified]**
Review that explicitly names *partner dance* and *handshake* as model tasks for physical human-human
interaction, proposing three categories: collaboration, assistance, education. Categorizes by
goal/role, not by grip configuration. The bridge document from haptics to dance.

**Mojtahedi, Whitsell, Artemiadis, Santello — "Communication and Inference of Intended Movement
Direction...," *Frontiers in Neurorobotics* 11:21, 2017.**
<https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurorobotics/articles/10.3389/fnbot.2017.00021/full>
**[search-verified]**
Leader/follower dyads communicate movement direction through the impedance relationship at a shared
handle (>83% inference accuracy). Grip is a shared handle/force channel; no configuration study.

**Human-human hand interactions aid balance during walking (2021)** and **Low-force hand interactions
induce gait changes (2024)** (Sawers/Ting-adjacent).
<https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8599825/>,

# Gap analysis

Four domains, each with a real body of work, each stopping in a different place before it reaches a
dance grip taxonomy.

## Dance itself stops at which-hand-holds-which

The formal dance codifications — NDCA definitions, Laird / ISTD technique books, WDSF, competition
rules — classify *which hand holds which* and *where the bodies are*. That is exactly the layer ZDL
already mathematized as set partitions. They stop there. The grip mechanics, when they exist at all,
live in three places: (1) scattered, unnamed single-grip specs in pedagogy — the WCS/lindy two-finger
"hook" with thumb up and free pinky, described independently at west-coast-swing.net and
lurklurk.org, both noticing its anisotropic stiffness (stiff in pull/push, free in rotation); (2)
informal community slang — "pistol grip," "spiderman," "the cup," "climb the wall," "hook," "death
grip" as anti-pattern; and (3) one genre, modern Western square dance, which standardizes a small grip
repertoire per call (forearm grip, hands-up crossed-palm, handshake, crossed hands) via CALLERLAB —
descriptively, not systematically. The one formal machine that *could* encode grips — Labanotation's
hand/finger notation (Hutchinson Guest & Kolff) — has never, as far as this survey found, been pointed
at classifying holds. Peer-reviewed dance science (Gentry & Murray-Smith; DeMers; Sawers/Ting)
formalizes the forces and information flowing *through* the grip and treats the grasp itself as an
unexamined given.

## Robotics assumes a rigid, passive, single-agent object

The grasp taxonomies everyone cites — Napier, Cutkosky, Feix, Kamakura, Bullock — rest on four shared
assumptions, and a partner's actively-gripping hand violates all four:

1. **Rigid, passive object.** Opposition type, virtual-finger assignment, Cutkosky's geometry
   branches are all defined against a static surface. A partner's hand is deformable *and actuated* —
   it changes its own shape and stiffness, so the "opposition surface" is a moving target.
2. **Single agent / unidirectional force.** One hand imposes force on an object that doesn't push
   back with intent. In a mutual hold both hands are simultaneously gripper and gripped, exchanging
   bidirectional, negotiated force.
3. **Asymmetric, fixed roles.** Even the two-agent extensions keep roles asymmetric and sequential:
   Krebs & Asfour's two hands cooperate on a *third* object; handover and self-handover have an
   explicit giver/receiver ordering. A dance handhold is role-symmetric — each hand is at once agent
   and object, and lead/follow force can swap continuously with no handover event.
4. **No sensing/agency in the gripped element.** The object can't anticipate or comply strategically;
   a partner's hand senses the grip and co-adapts, making the hold a closed-loop two-controller system
   with shared, ambiguous authority.

The nearest formal work is **Krebs & Asfour 2022** (bimanual) — and note the citation correction: an
earlier brief said "Krebs & Feix," which does not exist. The classification *variables* survive
(opposition, contact surface, power/precision, coordination, symmetry) but each needs a second copy
for the other hand, plus genuinely new axes — reciprocity, role symmetry, interlock geometry (who's
inside/outside) — that the object-grasp literature simply doesn't have.

## Haptics treats the grip as a black-box force channel

Across handshakes, dyadic haptics, hand-holding neuroscience, and physical human-robot interaction,
the mutual grip is reduced to *quantities*: contact force, grip strength, pressure, degree of closure,
impedance, coupling force, synchrony frequency, even brain coupling — sometimes literally a 1-DOF
stylus. No peer-reviewed work here classifies grip *geometry*. The partial exceptions prove the rule:
Knoop et al. (2017) measures contact area and finger positions but concludes grip location is nearly
invariant and builds no typology; Avelino et al. defines grip "types" that are force *levels*
(weak/medium/strong); Chaplin et al. rates "completeness of grip," a quality scalar. The *only*
sources that enumerate distinct geometric configurations (interlaced vs palm-clasp vs "paw") are
pop-psychology lifestyle articles with no measurement. That the handshake is almost always the same
configuration (palm-to-palm, thumb-web aligned) is probably *why* this field never needed a geometric
taxonomy — and exactly why dance, with its rich variety of holds, has no vocabulary waiting for it
here.

## Sport is closest in kind — and still not a general taxonomy

Sport gets nearest, from several directions at once, and no single source closes the gap:

- **Acroyoga / partner acrobatics** is closest *in kind*: hand-to-hand, reverse hand-to-hand,
  shake-hand, wrist-to-wrist are genuinely mutual, genuinely hand-on-hand, distinguished by exactly
  the variables a dance taxonomy needs — relative palm orientation, finger direction, wrist-vs-palm
  contact, load direction. But it lives in school manuals and web pedagogy, and it's weight-bearing,
  not light.
- **BJJ / wrestling** is closest *in vocabulary richness*: the self-clasp family (gable, S-grip,
  butterfly, ball-and-socket, seatbelt, Gokor) is the most finely differentiated set of named
  hand-hand closures anywhere, each with engineering rationale — but the two hands belong to one
  person, and the grips act on a limb or neck, not on another hand.
- **Judo** is the only domain with a real *peer-reviewed grip-classification literature* (41-study
  scoping review; laterality statistics over hundreds of matches), proving the research program is
  viable — but its grips are hand-on-cloth, coded by location × laterality × one/two-handed.
- **Arm wrestling** contributes the one fully codified *mutual palm-to-palm* grip in sport, specified
  down to thumb-knuckle visibility and assembled by a referee — but it's a single grip, not a
  taxonomy.

### Verdict

**No prior work classifies mutual hand-hand grasps.** Dance codifications stop at the set-partition
level ZDL already covered. Robotics classifies one hand on a passive object and breaks on all four
counts for hand-on-hand. Haptics measures forces through the grip and never its shape. Sport gets
closest but no source is a general taxonomy: acroyoga has the *forms*, grappling has the *clasp
descriptors*, judo has the *coding methodology*, arm wrestling has one *codified mutual grip*, and
Feix has the *dimensional discipline*. Their union nearly spans what dance needs — but nobody has
assembled it, and none of them covers dance's defining constraint: a hold that stays light,
releasable, and rotatable in the middle of a turn.

# Taxonomy sketch

**This is a sketch, not a model.** Sibling threads build the formal object; this section proposes the
axes and shows they can code real holds, so the formal work has somewhere to start. The move is to
take Feix's dimensions — power/precision, opposition type, virtual fingers, thumb position — and do
two things the object literature never had to: make each dimension *per-hand* (a grip can be power on
one side and precision on the other), and add the *mutual* axes (role symmetry, interlock, reciprocal
force) that only exist when the object grips back.

## Proposed axes

1. **Power ↔ precision, per hand.** Feix/Napier's core axis, but scored separately for each partner.
   A follower draping two fingers over a leader's offered hook is precision on her side; the leader
   supporting real leverage may be closer to power on his. One number per grip is not enough.

2. **Role symmetry.** Symmetric (both hands do the same thing — a handshake, arm-wrestling grip) vs
   asymmetric (one wraps, the other is wrapped — cupping, the WCS hook where one offers and one
   drapes). This is the axis every single-agent taxonomy lacks and every dance hold has. It connects
   upward to the set-partition layer: symmetry here is partly determined by which hands are joined.

3. **Enclosure / interlock geometry.** *How* the closure is formed: palm-to-palm clasp, hook-in-hook,
   interlaced fingers, cup (open, no closure), pistol (full-fist wrap). Borrowed straight from
   grappling's clasp family and dance slang. Partly discrete (you either interlace fingers or you
   don't) and partly continuous (how far the fingers wrap).

4. **Thumb engagement.** Engaged/wrapped vs floating/free. Grappling already knows this matters —
   the thumbless gable grip vs the thumb-wrapped S-grip — and dance's hook grip deliberately floats
   the thumb (thumb up, free) for quick release. A clean near-binary with real mechanical consequence.

5. **Palm orientation.** Palm-to-palm, palm-to-back, palm-to-side, on a rotation continuum. This is
   Feix's opposition type generalized to two hands, and it's what acro's "reverse hand-to-hand" vs
   "hand-to-hand" actually encodes (fingers pointing the same way vs opposite).

6. **Chirality / handedness.** L-R (handshake-compatible, mirrored) vs same-hand R-R or L-L. This is
   judo's ai-yotsu / kenka-yotsu laterality, and it connects directly back to the set-partition layer:
   which hands are joined constrains which grips are even reachable.

7. **Grip force / compliance regime.** The constraint no other domain has. A dance hold must stay
   *releasable and rotatable mid-turn* — light enough to slip out of instantly, compliant enough to
   spin inside during a turn (the WCS hook is engineered for exactly this: stiff in pull/push, free in
   rotation). Grappling wants the opposite (maximum lock); arm wrestling straps the hands together;
   acro bears full weight. Dance's whole point is a grip that can vanish on the next beat. This axis is
   continuous and is probably where the empirical work concentrates.

## Six dance holds, coded against the axes

Read across a row. This is a worked illustration, not validated data — the force/compliance and
per-hand power/precision columns in particular are informed estimates that need motion capture and
contact-area measurement to pin down.

| Hold | Power/precision (lead \| follow) | Role symmetry | Interlock geometry | Thumb | Palm orientation | Chirality | Force/compliance regime |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Handshake hold** | precision \| precision | symmetric | palm-to-palm clasp | wrapped, both | palm-to-palm | mirrored (L-R or R-R) | firm-ish, low compliance; not built to rotate |
| **WCS hook grip** | power-ish \| precision | asymmetric (offer / drape) | hook-in-hook (2 fingers) | floating (thumb up, free) | palm-down over offered hand | mirrored (L-R) | very light; stiff pull/push, free in rotation — the releasable ideal |
| **The cup** | power \| precision | asymmetric (cup / rest) | cup (open, no closure) | floating | palm-up cupping palm-down | mirrored | light, high compliance; releases instantly |
| **Interlaced-fingers hold** | precision \| precision | symmetric | interlaced fingers | engaged (fingers interwoven) | palm-to-palm | mirrored | secure, low compliance; *poor* for turns (locks rotation) — grappling rejects it for the same reason |
| **Hand-to-hand (acro)** | power \| power | symmetric | palm-to-palm, fingers wrap for correction | engaged | palm-to-palm, fingers aligned | same-direction stack | high force, weight-bearing; compliance sacrificed for support |
| **Double hand hold (two joins)** | precision \| precision | symmetric | two parallel clasps | wrapped or floating | palm-to-palm ×2 | mirrored ×2 | moderate; frame-defining, semi-releasable |

## What's continuous, what's discrete, what needs measuring

- **Discrete-ish:** interlock geometry (clasp / hook / interlace / cup / pistol are recognizably
  distinct), thumb engagement (near-binary), chirality (mirrored vs same-hand — inherited from the
  set-partition layer).
- **Continuous:** palm orientation (a rotation angle, not a category), power↔precision (a blend, and
  per-hand), and above all the **force/compliance regime** — light-to-locked with a directional
  stiffness profile (stiff along some axes, free along others), which is the axis dance most needs and
  no existing taxonomy measures.
- **Needs empirical work:** the per-hand power/precision split and the compliance regime are the two
  columns most obviously guessed above. The methods already exist in the literature — Kamakura's and
  Knoop's contact-area mapping (which surfaces touch, and how much), climbing biomechanics' joint-
  angle-plus-load method (Schweizer), and judo's video-coding methodology (location × laterality ×
  one/two-handed) — all pointed, for the first time, at light mutual dance holds instead of at objects,
  cloth, rock, or the near-invariant handshake. Motion capture plus contact-area sensing on real
  dancers is the obvious next instrument.

The honest summary: the pieces exist, scattered across four fields that never talked to each other.
Assembling them into a taxonomy of mutual hand-hand grasps — per-hand Feix dimensions, plus role
symmetry, interlock, and a compliance regime nobody else needed — is open work, and it's ours to do.
